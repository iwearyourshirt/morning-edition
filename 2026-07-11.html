Privacy Watch
03 · Surveillance

EU Parliament greenlights Chat Control 1.0.

Nearly four hundred points and two hundred and twenty comments for the news that the European Parliament has approved “Chat Control 1.0” — the message-scanning regime that privacy advocates have spent years fighting. Former MEP Patrick Breyer, the law’s most persistent opponent, delivered the verdict in the headline itself: “Our children lose out.”

The pattern is familiar by now: scanning of private communications, justified on child-protection grounds, opposed by nearly every cryptographer who has looked at it. The HN thread is running heavily toward the view that a scanned channel is not a private channel, whatever the scanning is for. Wherever you land, this is the largest legislature in the world normalizing the inspection of private messages — and precedents like this travel well across borders.

Applies to you if……you care where end-to-end encryption is headed. Nothing to change today — but this is the file to watch, because platform-level scanning mandates in one large market have a habit of becoming defaults everywhere.

John Deere owners win the right to repair their own machines.

Twelve hundred points. Twelve hundred. Under an FTC settlement, John Deere owners will get the right to repair their own equipment — the culmination of a fight that farmers, tinkerers, and repair advocates have waged for the better part of a decade, ever since it became clear that a modern tractor is a computer with wheels and a licensing agreement.

The right-to-repair movement has spent years collecting small wins — state laws here, a manufacturer memorandum there. A federal settlement against the movement’s single most symbolic adversary is a different order of victory. The thread reads like a decade of pent-up vindication, two hundred and forty-nine comments deep.

06

Spider venom kills the bees’ worst enemy — and spares the bees.

The varroa mite is the closest thing beekeeping has to a nemesis: a parasite implicated in colony collapse worldwide, and one that has proven maddeningly hard to poison without poisoning the bees it rides on. Australian researchers now report that a compound derived from spider venom kills varroa mites while leaving honeybees unharmed — a selectivity that existing treatments have chased for decades.

Two hundred and thirty-seven points for the sheer poetry of it: the spider, the bee’s ancient predator, conscripted as its bodyguard. Nature keeps writing better plot twists than we do.

Engineering · Deep Dive

Meta’s custom chip gives old RAM a second life.

Hyperscalers retire hardware on a schedule, and the memory inside is usually collateral — perfectly functional silicon scrapped because the platform around it moved on. Meta’s answer, detailed in a Network World report doing numbers on HN today, is a custom bridge chip that lets older RAM slot into new servers, decoupling the memory’s useful life from the chassis it happened to ship in.

Two hundred points and a hundred and twenty-eight comments, many circling the same observation: at Meta’s scale, “reduce, reuse” stops being a slogan and becomes a line item worth designing silicon for. It’s the rare infrastructure story where the frugality is the innovation.

Leap seconds to be added at the end of December 2026

The IERS — the international body that referees the disagreement between atomic clocks and the actual spinning Earth — has issued its ruling: no leap second this December. A bulletin that says, in effect, nothing will happen, and seventy comments materialized anyway.

Because nothing happening is the story. The planet has been spinning slightly fast for years, no leap second has been needed in nearly a decade, and somewhere in the future lurks the negative leap second — a minute with fifty-nine seconds — which essentially no software on Earth has ever been tested against. Every one of these bulletins is a stay of execution, and the engineers in the thread know it.
