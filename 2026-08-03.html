The Big One · Sharp Essay

Don’t be a meat proxy.

01

Six hundred points and climbing in three hours — that’s the fastest-moving thing on Hacker News this morning, and it’s an essay with a title built to sting. The provocation: if your job has been reduced to ferrying output between an AI and the rest of the world — copying, pasting, forwarding, nodding — you have become the meat in the middle of someone else’s pipeline.

The comment section, 263 strong, is doing the thing HN does best when an essay hits a nerve: half the room defending the human-in-the-loop as the last line of judgment, the other half quietly admitting the essay describes their Tuesday. For anyone building skill-based AI workflows — where the point is to design the system rather than relay for it — this reads less like an attack and more like a mission statement.

Heads Up · Privacy
03 · Your Data, Your Chats

What are you pasting into that AI chat, exactly?

A small new open-source project called PISIGuard surfaced on the front page this morning with a simple premise: sit between you and your AI chats, and catch personal or sensitive information before it leaves your machine. It’s early — a GitHub repo, a handful of points — but the problem it names is not small.

Anyone who lives in AI tools all day develops a reflexive paste habit: logs, emails, contracts, client names, API keys riding along in a stack trace. The tooling for noticing what you’re sharing has badly lagged the tooling for sharing it. Whether this particular project is the answer matters less than the reminder.

Applies to you if……you paste real client material, credentials, or personal data into AI chats without a second look. Worth an occasional audit of your own paste reflexes — especially API keys and tokens hiding inside logs and config files.

Hacker Culture · Audacious Engineering

Kakehashi wants to run your Mac apps on Linux.

An experimental project called Kakehashi — Japanese for “bridge” — is building a userspace layer to run macOS binaries on ARM Linux. It is Wine’s audacity pointed in a direction almost nobody has dared: not Windows-to-Linux, but Mac-to-Linux, on the architecture Apple itself now runs on.

Two hundred twenty-one points and a comment thread rich with the particular joy HN reserves for beautiful, borderline-quixotic systems work. It is experimental, incomplete, and exactly the kind of project that either quietly dies or becomes indispensable infrastructure five years from now. Both outcomes are worth watching.

Sharp Essay · Working With AI

“Retype the code your LLM writes. All of it.”

A developer proposes a discipline that sounds absurd until it doesn’t: instead of accepting LLM-generated code with a keystroke, manually retype it — every line — as a way of preventing what they call cognitive debt. The retyping isn’t the point; the forced comprehension is. You cannot type a function you haven’t read, and you cannot read a function without forming an opinion about it. A deliberately friction-full answer to the meat-proxy problem at the top of this issue — the two essays are in conversation whether their authors know it or not.

Essay · Developer Psychology

Tools encode trust. That’s why we can’t quit them.

Why do developers get so irrationally attached to their tools? The Stack Overflow blog offers an answer with more explanatory power than the usual “habit”: a tool you’ve used for years is a ledger of every time it didn’t betray you. Switching isn’t learning new keybindings — it’s zeroing out an accumulated trust balance and starting over with a stranger.

Two hundred thirteen points and 117 comments, many quietly confessing to editors they’ve used since college. It’s a useful lens for the current moment, when everyone’s toolchain is being renegotiated by AI at once: the resistance you feel — or watch others feel — isn’t stubbornness. It’s an actuarial judgment about trust, and it deserves to be argued with on those terms.

A blogging platform that only accepts handwriting.

Handwritten.blog is exactly what it says: a publishing platform where posts are handwritten, not typed. A hundred-plus points and a comment thread torn between “this is a gimmick” and “this is the most sincere thing I’ve seen all month.” Both can be true. The second one matters more.

A place you can only visit with ssh.

The entire pitch fits in a shell prompt: type ssh ssh.place and you’re there. A shared space that lives entirely inside the terminal — no browser, no app, no account creation funnel. A hundred seventeen points and 71 comments of people delighting in the constraint.

Weird & Wonderful · Retrocomputing
10

Someone ran a language model on a 6502.

6502
The 1975 processor — of Apple II and NES fame — now running an autoregressive language model

The MOS 6502 shipped in 1975, powered the Apple II and the NES, and addresses memory in kilobytes. This week, someone got an autoregressive language model — a BitNet-style network — running on one. It is slow beyond any practical use, which is entirely beside the point.

The point is what the exercise reveals: strip a language model down far enough and the mathematical core is small enough to fit inside a machine from the Ford administration. Everything else — the scale, the speed, the trillion-token training runs — is amplification of an idea simple enough to run on fifty-year-old silicon. A hundred eleven points of pure, uncut hacker joy to close the issue.
